<Initial text="Saints Simon and Jude" />

**October 28**<br/>
Apostles (1st century AD)<br/>
*Feast*

## Morning Prayer

[Simon](/people/saints/apostles/simon) the Canaanite, one of the twelve apostles (Mark 3:18), is also called Simon the Zealot (Luke 6:15). This latter term, which is peculiar to [St. Luke](/people/saints/evangelists/luke), is the Greek equivalent for the Aramaic term “Canaanite”, which does not signify a descendant of Canaan, but a member of the Jewish sect or faction of the Zealots, who were conspicuous for their fierce advocacy of the Mosaic Law.

It is very uncertain where Simon preached. Moses of Chorene, an Armenian writer of the 5th century, says that he preached in Persia. But he adds that “some relate that a certain Simon the Apostle perished at Bosphorus in Iberia. But whether it be true I know not, nor why he should have gone there.” [St. Isidore of Seville](/people/saints/latin-fathers/isidore-of-seville) places him in Egypt, but neither Copts nor Ethiopians have any commemoration of him.

As no certainty exists as to where he preached, so also is it doubtful whether he died a martyr’s death or not, and where he died. The Menology of Basil II says he preached at Edessa and there died peacefully. The Latin Martyrologies generally say that he was martyred in Persia.

In the year 1204, an Armenian bishop came to Cologne, stating that he had made the journey from his native land for the purpose of venerating the Three Kings. He brought with him an arm of St. Simon the Zealot and gave it to Bruno, provost of Bonn, who in turn gave the precious relic over to the Sayn Abbey in the diocese of Trèves.

In art, St. Simon is represented with a long saw, as he is supposed to have been sawn asunder.[^1]

## Evening Prayer

In the [Gospel of St. Matthew](/bible/new-testament/gospels/matthew) and the [Gospel of St. Mark](/bible/new-testament/gospels/mark), Lebbaeus, “whose surname was Thaddaeus” (Matt. 10:3; Mark 3:18), is mentioned among the twelve apostles. In the [Gospel of St. Luke](/bible/new-testament/gospels/luke), he is referred to as “Judas of James” (Luke 6:16). In the [Gospel of St. John](/bible/new-testament/gospels/john), he is referred to as “Judas (not Iscariot)” (John 14:22). There can be no doubt that Judas is the same as Lebbaeus or Thaddaeus. But much difference of opinion has existed from the earliest times as to the right interpretation of “Judas of James”. The generally received interpretation is that the King James Version is right in translating “Judas of James” as “Judas the brother of James.” The canonical Epistle is headed “Jude the servant of Jesus Christ, and brother of James” (Jude 1:1). [St. Jerome](/people/saints/latin-fathers/jerome-of-stridon) also calls Judas “the brother of James.” [St. James](/people/saints/apostles/james-the-less) is called by St. Paul in his Epistle to the Galatians “the brother of the Lord” (Gal. 1:19), that is, his cousin. This is confirmed by [Eusebius](/people/early-christian-writers/eusebius) and by St. Jerome. [Papias](/people/saints/apostolic-fathers/papias-of-hierapolis), a contemporary of [St. Polycarp](/people/saints/apostolic-fathers/polycarp-of-smyrna), speaking of the family of our Lord, says, “Mary the wife of Cleopas, or Alphaeus, who was the mother of James the Bishop and Apostle, and of Simon and Thaddaeus, and of a certain Joseph.” Consequently, we arrive at this conclusion: St. Simon, [St. Jude](/people/saints/apostles/jude), and St. James were the sons of Cleopas and Mary. They are called our Lord’s brethren, that is, they were cousins of our Lord.

St. Jude is said to have preached in Mesopotamia and at Edessa. Tradition states that he preached at Antaradi and Laodicea, Palmyra, Callinicum, and thence to have pushed eastward into Edom. That St. Jude was a martyr is generally admitted in the West. Pseudo-Hippolytus says he died at Berytus.

Eusebius quotes from Hegesippus a curious passage related to the children of St. Jude: “There were yet living of the family of our Lord, the grandchildren of Judas, the brother of our Lord, according to the flesh. These were reported as being of the family of David, and were brought to Domitian by Revocatus. For this emperor was as much alarmed at the appearance of Christ as Herod. He put the question whether they were of David’s race, and they confessed that they were. He then asked what property they had, and how much money they owned. And both of them answered that they had between them only nine thousand denarii, and this not in silver, but in the value of a piece of land containing thirty-nine acres; from which they raised their taxes, and supported themselves by their own labour. Then they also showed their hands, exhibiting the hardness of their bodies, and the callosity formed by incessant labour on their hands, as evidence of their own labour. When asked also respecting Christ and his kingdom, what was its nature, and when and where it was to appear, they replied that it was not a temporal nor an earthly kingdom, but celestial and angelic; that it would appear at the end of the world, when coming in glory Christ would judge the quick and dead, and give every one according to his works. Upon which Domitian, despising them, made no reply, but treating them with contempt, as simpletons, commanded them to be dismissed, and by a decree ordered the persecution to cease. Thus delivered, they ruled the churches both as witnesses and relatives of the Lord. When peace was established, they continued living even to the times of Trajan.”

[St. Bernard](/people/saints/medieval/bernard-of-clairvaux) was buried with some fragments of the relics of St. Jude on his breast, whence obtained nobody knows. When St. Bernard was dug up and enshrined, these relics were placed apart at Clairvaux. In art, St. Jude is represented with a club (a sign of his martyrdom), a cross, and sometimes a carpenter’s square; a boat or ship is often near him.[^2]

**July 27**<br/>
(1st century AD)<br/>
*Commemoration*

## Morning Prayer

[St. Martha](/people/saints/biblical/martha), the sister of [Lazarus](/people/saints/biblical/lazarus) and [Mary](/people/saints/biblical/mary-of-bethany), lived with her brother and sister at Bethany. All we know of her is from what is recorded in the Gospels. The two sisters seem to have been better known among the Jews than the brother; this the language of [St. John](/people/saints/apostles/john) implies. Lazarus is said to be "of Bethany, the village of Mary and her sister Martha" (John 11:1). From this, and from the order of the three names in St. John (John 11:5), we may reasonably infer that Lazarus was the youngest of the family.

A feast was prepared for our Lord in their house. Martha serves, and Mary takes upon herself that which was the special duty of a hostess towards an honoured guest. From the account in St. John's Gospel (John 12:2) we should have supposed that the brother and sister were the givers of the feast. But in the Gospels of [St. Matthew](/people/saints/apostles/matthew) (Matt. 26:6) and [St. Mark](/people/saints/evangelists/mark) (Mark 14:3), the same facts are recorded as happening in the house of Simon the Leper. But a leper, as such, would have been compelled to live a separate life, and certainly could not have given a feast and received a multitude of guests. It may be supposed, therefore, that Simon was a man who had been healed of his leprosy, and it is not improbable that he was the father of Lazarus, Martha, and Mary. Or it may be that he had been stricken with leprosy, [leaving] his children free to act for themselves. A comparison of St. Matthew (Matt 26:6), St. Mark (Mark 14:3), with [St. Luke](/people/saints/evangelists/luke) (Luke 7:39-40) suggests the probability that Simon the Leper was also the Pharisee Simon, mentioned by the other evangelists, and this would explain the fact of the friendship which existed between the sisters of Lazarus and the members of that party in Jerusalem. All the circumstances mentioned by St. John point to their having enjoyed wealth and social position above the average.

The account we receive of St. Martha indicates a character devout, after the customary Jewish type of devotion, sharing in the Messianic hopes, and accepting Jesus as the Christ. When she first comes before us, as receiving her Lord into her house, she is "cumbered with much serving" (Luke 10:40), is "careful and troubled about many things" (Luke 10:41). She needs the reproof: "One thing is needful" (Luke 10:42); but her love, though imperfect in form, is yet true, and she too, no less than Lazarus and Mary, has the distinction of being one whom Jesus loved (cf. John 11:3). Her position is obviously that of an elder sister.

After the raising of Lazarus we hear of our Lord again supping at the house which, though it still bore the father's name, was the dwelling of the sisters and their brother. Lazarus is there, and Martha serves, no longer jealously, and Mary pours out her love in the costly offering of the spikenard ointment (John 12:1-3).

## Evening Prayer

The legend of St. Martha ... forms the subject of a curious religious romance attributed to Hrabanus Maurus, Archbishop of Mainz, who died in 856 AD. ... After the martyrdom of [St. Stephen](/people/saints/biblical/stephen), Lazarus and his two sisters escaped from the persecution, which raged against the Church, into Galilee. ... When the first persecution had passed over, they returned to Jerusalem, and remained there till the second persecution in 45 AD. ... Barbarians placed her and all her company in an old boat without mast or oars on the Mediterranean, that they might perish at sea. It passed [safely] across [the] wide tracts of sea, which expand between Palestine and Sicily, and between Sicily and Provence, and [miraculously] arrived at Marseilles, which was then one of the principal ports and celebrated cities of Gaul.

Between Arles and Avignon, in the midst of small woods and the gravelly banks of the Rhone, was a desert infested by ferocious beasts and venomous reptiles ... There, among other poisonous creatures, lived a terrible dragon, of incredible length and of enormous size. Its mouth exhaled pestilential vapours; from its eyes flames issued; its jaws, armed with crooked teeth, uttered strident whistlings and horrible roars. It tore to rags whatever fell under its claws, or came between its teeth; every living being that approached died of the stink of its breath. The number of beasts and shepherds it devoured is incredible; incredible also is the number of men who died of its poisonous breath.

One day, the very holy Martha was announcing the word of God to a crowd of people assembled on the spot, whose conversation turned [to the dragon]; and some of those present [appealed to her] with devout supplications; [others tempted her by saying], "If the Christ, whom that holy woman preaches, has any power, let her show it now, and deliver us from the dragon." Martha said to them, "If you will believe, nothing is impossible to them that will believe." Then the people having promised to believe, the saint preceded the grateful crowd, and with a firm pace, walked to the dragon's den, and, making the sign of the cross, she appeased its ferocity; then tied her girdle round the neck of the monster, and looking at the people who gazed from a distance with wonder, said, "What ails you?" She cried, "Why do you tremble? Here is the serpent, a prisoner, and you hesitate still. Approach boldly in the name of the Lord, and tear the venomous monster to pieces." Encouraged by her words, the multitude came up, and falling on the dragon, rent it in a thousand pieces. A city named Tarascon was afterwards founded on the spot, and took St. Martha as its patron.

The reputed house of Martha is now the Church of St. Martha, a church with work of the 12th century in it, but for the most part dating from the 14th. In the crypt beneath the nave, which is earlier, is the shrine and tomb of St. Martha, ornamented with her recumbent effigy in white marble, [well] executed, but modern. Against the walls, the history of St. Martha is represented in a series of bas-reliefs in a sad state of [damage]. In the floor is a well, the water of which is said to rise and fall with the Rhone.[^1]

<Initial text="Saint Jerome" />

**September 30**<br/>
Priest (c. 419 AD)<br/>
*Commemoration*

## Morning Prayer

[St. Jerome](/people/saints/latin-fathers/jerome-of-stridon) was born at Stridon, on the confines of Dalmatia and Pannonia, a town destroyed by the Goths in the lifetime of the Saint, thought to be a little place called Strigova between the Mur and the Drave, near the point of contact of Croatia, Styria, and Hungary. It is a village consisting at present of some thirty cottages with a chapel dedicated to St. Jerome; and the house in which he was born is there pointed out, perched on a hill. A miraculous spring issues from the base of the hill, called the fountain of St. Jerome. Over the door of the house is placed an inscription: "Here was born St. Jerome, Doctor of the Church." St. Jerome was born about the year 331. His parents were Christians, of honourable family, and wealthy. He had a brother named Paulinian and a sister, whose name we do not know. The young Jerome was instructed in rhetoric and law to prepare him for the legal profession. He continued his studies in Rome. There he attended the courts to study oratory. At the same time he was known to visit the catacombs and prowl along the dark avenues, studying the walls honeycombed with cells in which lay the bodies of martyrs and confessors.

Jerome had not left Rome in 363, when Julian the Apostate was killed. He was not idle during his stay there, for in addition to studying oratory and grammar, he busily collected a library. His moral conduct does not, however, seem to have been exemplary. He repeatedly laments the fact that in his youth he fell into sins of the flesh. At that time he was not baptized. He had lost, he says in one place, the innocence given to him at his nativity; a new innocence had been given him at his second birth, which he had hoped not to be despoiled of. Yet even his baptismal garment he had stained, and in one of his letters to [Damasus](/people/saints/latin-fathers/damasus-of-rome) the Pope, he sadly says that having been baptized by the Spirit, and having defiled his robe of innocence, he must look forward to the baptism of fire, in which its stains might be burnt out.

From Rome St. Jerome went to Trèves and to the Rhine, dropping down the Moselle. At Trèves he copied two books of [St. Hilary](/people/saints/latin-fathers/hilary-of-poitiers), and this seems to have marked the turn of his mind to sacred studies. There also he made acquaintance with Rufinus, a friendship which was not, however, destined to last through life. From Trèves he returned south, and remained some time at Aquileia with his friend Rufinus, from about 370 to 372. Jerome left Aquileia for Antioch in 372, where he was cast on the bed of sickness. Whilst Jerome lay sick at Antioch, his friend Heliodorus came to see him from Jerusalem, and informed him that Rufinus was then in Egypt. Heliodorus was bent on retiring into the desert, and embracing an eremitical life, and on taking Jerome with him; but to this Jerome would not consent. He perhaps felt himself too ill to endure the austerities of a hermit's life. In 374 he was still at Antioch, and suffering from bad health, and an attack of fever, which reduced him to a condition of great emaciation and exhaustion.

The season was Lent, and, although he was ill, he did not consider himself exempt from observing the fast. Thus he was reduced to a condition subject to strange hallucinations. He amused himself on his bed with reading Plautus, but chiefly Cicero, who was his favourite author. Whilst so doing one day, he fell into a fit of unconsciousness, and thought he saw himself standing before the throne of God. The Judge asked, "Who art thou?" "A Christian," answered Jerome in his trance. "Thou liest," answered the Almighty; "thou art a Ciceronian." Jerome thought he staggered back in dismay, and sank on his face, covered with confusion. When he recovered consciousness, he promised to cast aside all his heathen books, and resolved to devote his whole attention to the study of divinity. In the ardour of his zeal he wrote to Theodosius, an abbot in Cilicia, to announce his desire to embrace the monastic life. He retired into the desert in 374 AD.

## Evening Prayer

From his desert cell, St. Jerome wrote to his friends for more books to assist him in passing the time, which in his solitude dragged somewhat heavily. There he wrote the life of [Paul](/people/saints/desert-fathers/paul-of-thebes), the first hermit, and sent it to Paul of Concordia, a friend, near Aquileia. The place of his retreat was near Maronia in Chalcis, a spot which he describes as "burnt up with the heat of the sun." There, according to his own account, his skin was scorched brown, he slept on the soil, his bones protruded, he grew ragged and miserable in appearance. Being sorely troubled by sensual thoughts, he wisely devoted his mind to the hard task of learning Hebrew, so that it might be kept fully occupied, and not have time for rambling to unlawful subjects. "What labours did I undertake in learning that alphabet and acquiring those harsh and aspirated words! What difficulties I had to undergo! How often I despaired! How often I gave it up, and yet again buckled to my task! ... But, thanks be to God, now I pluck sweet fruit of letters off that bitter road."

It was whilst Jerome was in the desert that the Meletian schism broke out. Antioch had at the same time two bishops, and the church there had been broken into two parties adhering to the rival prelates. The Eastern bishops recognised Meletius, the Roman Church Paulinus. The East at this time was distracted with questions as to the term "hypostasis", whether it was applicable to the Three Persons of the Trinity. Syrian monks invaded Jerome's solitude, and importuned him to confess the three hypostases. He answered that he fully believed in the Three Persons, but could not take "hypostasis" to mean anything but "essence". They insisted that he should accept the phrase as well as the doctrine. In his perplexity, he applied to Pope Damasus of Rome. This appeal was made about the beginning of 377, and in the following year arrived the answer to it by Damasus, addressed to Paulinus. In around 397, St. Jerome left his retreat and came to Antioch, where he was ordained priest by Paulinus. In around 380, St. Jerome went to Constantinople, where he had the gratification of hearing [St. Gregory of Nazianzus](/people/saints/cappadocian-fathers/gregory-of-nazianzus) give sermons and instructions. He remained two or three years at Constantinople, arrested by the eloquence of St. Gregory, and occupied in translating into Latin and continuing the chronicle of [Eusebius](/people/early-christian-writers/eusebius), and in translating the homilies of [Origen](/people/early-christian-writers/origen-of-alexandria) on Jeremiah and Ezekiel.

Thence, in 382, he went to Rome. Damasus formed a high opinion of his learning and merits, and constituted him his secretary. He consulted him on scriptural points, which his Hebrew scholarship enabled him to pronounce an opinion upon with authority. He also, at the request of the pope, corrected the Latin version of the New Testament (then in common use), and then went on to revise the Psalter by the Hebrew. St. Jerome had at first been greatly esteemed by the Roman Christians. But the roughness and bitterness of his nature, his open scorn for the vanities and pettiness of some of the clergy, and his success in drawing Roman women like Marcella and Paula to the monastic life, had aroused such hostility against him that even his outward appearance, his "walk and smile," furnished occasion for slander. Disgusted, Jerome left Rome in 385 AD. He set sail for Antioch, where he saw Paulinus, and thence started during the cold of winter for Jerusalem, where he visited the sacred places.

Almost directly after he left Rome, Paula, the wealthy widow whom he had converted to monastic principles, finding Rome no longer endurable, started with the intention of overtaking him. This she probably succeeded in doing at Cyprus, and she was able under his directions to visit with him all the spots consecrated by the Gospel narrative in the Holy Land. Thence they went together to Egypt to study the manner of life of the hermits and obtain hints for the establishment of similar religious societies in Palestine. Having acquired the information they needed, Jerome and Paula returned to the Holy Land and settled at Bethlehem, not to leave it; and there Jerome built for himself a small monastery, and Paula a convent. In the poor and narrow cell of his monastic house, this glorious cenobite accomplished the translation and commentary of the Scriptures. He produced thus the Vulgate, which has made him "the master of Christian prose for all following ages." He died on September 30, 419, doubtless in full Christian peace. St. Jerome was buried at Bethlehem in his cave, and his body remained there till the 13th century, when it is said to have been translated to the Church of Santa Maria Maggiore at Rome.[^1]

<Initial text="Saint Augustine" />

**August 28**<br/>
Bishop (430 AD)<br/>
*Commemoration*

## Morning Prayer

[St. Augustine](/people/saints/latin-fathers/augustine-of-hippo) was born at Thagaste in Northern Africa on the 13th of November, 354 AD. His father’s name was Patricius, his mother was the holy and gentle Monica. Patricius was an [ill-tempered] man, a pagan, but [decent by his own standards], loving his wife and children. Augustine, like other boys full of [youthful energy], was not fond of books; as for Greek, he hated it, and would not learn it. [He later recounts,] “I perhaps knew that *holon* did not mean ‘one’ but ‘all’, and that *kath’ holon*, whence the Catholic name is derived, meant ‘according to all’.” In [later] years he was obliged to extend his knowledge of Greek. He was sent early to Carthage, and there learned rhetoric. The theatres, racecourse, and amphitheatre delighted him, and corrupted the simplicity of his boyish mind. At Carthage he [formed a relationship] with a young woman, and at the age of eighteen became a father.

The reading of Cicero’s *Hortensius* excited his growing mind to the study of philosophy. Manichaeism offered to him a clear system of belief … But he found that the Manichaeans were great rather in pulling to pieces the religious faith of others than in supporting their own doctrines with satisfactory proofs; and what especially dissatisfied him was the contemptuous way in which they threw doubt on the historical existence of Christ. … The early instructions of his mother were not to be eradicated by a light breath of doubt such as this. Faustus of Mileve, an eminent Manichaean, came to Carthage. Augustine listened to him with eagerness, admitted his eloquence, but was dissatisfied with his logic.

In 383, Augustine went to Rome to teach rhetoric against his mother’s advice. At Rome he lived with Manichaeans, … [but] soon found that Manichaeism admitted of luxury or asceticism with easy indifference. … It was the insufficiency of Manichaeism to supply man with an ethical code which finally revolted the intellect of Augustine. Having shaken himself free from Manichaeism, Augustine joined himself to the [Academic skeptics], [but] in the conflict of philosophies he could not find rest. He felt like a man in a forest, through which long aisles betwixt tree [trunks] led, all offering a road, and all leading to points whence radiated similar long passages of gloom and bewilderment.

From Rome he went to Milan in 384 to teach rhetoric, and there fell under the charm of the presence and teaching of [St. Ambrose](/people/saints/latin-fathers/ambrose-of-milan). Another, a softer, even a holier presence came also to exert its influence on the young rhetorician. … He attended church with his [close] friend Alypius, listened to the discourses of Ambrose, and began to read the sacred Scriptures. An African named Ponticianus, an officer in the imperial palace, called one day at the house of Augustine, and [told him the story of [St. Anthony](/people/saints/desert-fathers/anthony-the-great) and how his life had inspired others to forsake the world and devote themselves wholly to God]. This story shook the soul of Augustine to its foundation. The Gospel was not a mere historic narrative, Christianity not one theory of man’s relation to God among many, Jesus Christ not a founder of a system only; the Gospel, Christianity, [and] Jesus Christ were living powers, today as mighty as [centuries] ago. … [Augustine quickly got up] and left the [house], scarcely knowing where he went … He came to a fig tree, and flung himself down on the turf under its shade. Then, without restraint, he gave vent to his emotion; the tears streamed from his eyes upon the grass, and his breast shook with sobs. At that moment he heard a child’s voice singing, “Take up and read! Take up and read!” With tottering steps, Augustine returned to the [house] and, opening the scroll, his eyes fell on the words: “Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying, but put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh to fulfil the lusts thereof” (Rom. 13:13–14).

## Evening Prayer

On Easter Eve, April 25, 387, [St. Augustine] was baptized by St. Ambrose at Milan. Alypius was baptized with him, and Augustine’s son Adeodatus. … After the series of lessons usual on Holy Saturday, the Great Sabbath, [they] were led to the font, where a priest and a deacon attended on St. Ambrose. With faces turned westward, they renounced “the devil and all his works, the world, its luxury, its pleasures;” and then turned eastward in recognition of Christ. Ambrose then performed the benediction of the font. Each candidate descended into it, was asked, “Dost thou believe in God the Father Almighty?”, answered, “I believe,” and was immersed in the water. [Each] professed in like manner his faith “in our Lord Jesus Christ and his cross, and in the Holy Spirit,” and was immersed a second and third time. Ambrose then anointed the head of each with consecrated oil, and a prayer that this might be unto life eternal. … They saw the altar in its fair array, with lamps burning around it, decked for the Easter Communion; and were at once admitted to the highest privileges of the Church. [Later] it was said that on this occasion from the swelling breasts of Ambrose and Augustine sprang that inspired triumph song, the [*Te Deum*](/liturgy/music/chants/te-deum).

[After the passing of his mother Monica, Augustine returned to Rome.] In 388, Augustine … wrote his work *On the Morals of the Manichaeans*, and began [writing] *On Free Will*. Towards the close of the year he returned to Africa, … [where he wrote] *On Music* and *On True Religion* … In 391, against his will, he was withdrawn from his place of retreat, and ordained priest by Valerius, Bishop of Hippo. … Valerius was very old, and utterly incapacitated for discharging his episcopal duties. He had set his heart on having Augustine for his successor. … The date of [Augustine’s] consecration [as bishop] cannot be fixed with certainty, but it was [likely] in 395 AD, when he was forty-one.

[Donatism](/glossary/historical-terms#donatism) was a constant thorn in the side of Augustine; he combated it with no less vigour than he displayed against Manichaeism. … He wrote at this time his great book *On the Trinity*, and the elaborate work *Against Faustus the Manichaean*, whom in his *Confessions* he had characterized as that “great snare of the devil” … In the summer of 411, the [bishops] of Western Africa held a conference with the Donatists at Carthage, and … [Emperor] Honorius commissioned his secretary, Marcellinus, to preside. … The conference was long, recriminative, and ended in neither party convincing the other. Marcellinus then drew up a sentence, ordering the suppression of Donatist [assemblies] … Whilst [Augustine] was absorbed by the business of the conference, he [encountered] Pelagius … [Pelagianism](/glossary/historical-terms#pelagianism), he said, was reducible to two principles: the denial of supernatural grace and the denial of original sin. … His controversy with Pelagius occupied Augustine till 418. … In 427, Augustine carried out a long-cherished [plan] of reviewing all his treatises, and correcting whatever might displease his ripened judgment. This process of rehandling was applied (in two books which bear the name of *Retractiones*) to ninety-three treatises; the letters were reserved for a subsequent revision.

Augustine was taken away before Africa fell under the sway of the Arian Vandals. Hippo, one of the three cities which had as yet escaped the common ruin, was besieged by the Vandals in June, 430. One day, Augustine, while at table, announced to the other bishops who had taken shelter in Hippo that he had prayed God either to deliver the city or to strengthen his servants to bear his will, or at least to take him out of this world. … He ceased not to preach and work, till in August he was prostrated by fever; and, as he used to say that even approved Christians and priests ought to die as penitents, he excluded his friends from his room (except at certain hours), caused the penitential psalms to be written out and fixed on the wall opposite his bed, and repeated them with many tears … He expired on August 28, 430 AD, in his seventy-seventh year.[^1]

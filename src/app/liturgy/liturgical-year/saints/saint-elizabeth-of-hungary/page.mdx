<Initial text="Saint Elizabeth of Hungary" />

**November 19**<br/>
Religious (1231 AD)<br/>
*Commemoration*

## Morning Prayer

Hermann, Landgrave of Thuringia and Hesse, was married first to the daughter of the Elector of Saxony; and when she died childless, he married Sophia of Bavaria, by whom he had four sons: Ludwig, Hermann, Henry, and Conrad. Their son Hermann died young. One day during the year 1211, as Hermann, Landgrave of Thuringia, sat among his court poets and musicians in his castle, the Wartburg, the renowned poet Klingsor of Hungaria announced to him that on that very night, Gertrude of Meran, consort of Andrew II of Hungary, would give birth to a daughter, the destined bride of his eldest son, Ludwig. This daughter, [Elizabeth](/people/saints/medieval/elizabeth-of-hungary), was at once demanded in marriage for his son by the landgrave, and she was carried in a silver cradle to the Wartburg, attended by the gallant knight Walther von Vargila. The arrival of the baby bride was honoured with festivities; and as a symbol of their union, the two little children were rocked to sleep in the same cradle. The children grew up together from infancy in much love, regarding each other as brother and sister.

In the year 1215, Hermann the landgrave died at Gotha, and his body was brought to Eisenach, and buried in the church of [St. Catherine](/people/saints/early/catherine-of-alexandria). Ludwig, his eldest son, succeeded him, he was then fifteen, his little bride was only nine or ten years old. The young landgrave was a boy of great promise. His chaplain Berchthold says of him, “He was a youth of nobleness of character and of delightful holiness. As he now came to the full bloom of youth, he seemed full of all virtues, great goodness, and mild compassion … Although in the first years of succeeding his father he had to endure much opposition and hostility, yet his natural virtue and sincerity did not suffer, for his mind was ever musing on heavenly things.” Elizabeth was not kindly treated by Sophia, the mother of the young landgrave. She was prejudiced against the child, and thought her son might make a more advantageous match. Ludwig stood chivalrously by her, and refused to listen to proposals for a dissolution of engagement. But he could not always be at home, and in his absence she suffered. Her heart clung with intense love to the handsome boy who was affianced to her. She had no one else to love; her mother had been assassinated in 1212; she had no friends in the Wartburg, and she turned instinctively to the Church, the home of the sorrowful. Elizabeth was “earnest in her conduct, modest in all her ways, kindly in speech, fervent in her prayers, and overflowing in her charity to poor people, peacefully disposed towards her attendants, considerate of her maids, and full of virtues and godly love at all times.”

As a little child, she had been driven to find playmates among the poor peasant girls; now, grown nearly to womanhood, finding only cold looks in the castle, she sought the grateful smiles on the faces of the sick whom she ministered to in their cottages. In 1220, the Landgrave Ludwig celebrated his marriage with Elizabeth. The wedding was celebrated with great splendour, and lasted three days. As soon as the festivities were over, she returned to her favourite occupation of looking after the poor. In plain clothes she visited the meanest cottages, sat by the sick on their beds, and gave them the food and warm coverings she had herself brought them. Never weary in her holy work, never shrinking from the poorest beggar, never repelling the most ungrateful of those whom she assisted, she became an object of wonder and admiration to the suffering and needy of the whole neighbourhood.

The year 1225 was one of great distress. Heavy winds beat down the corn, and shook the grain out of the ears; then followed rain, and the wheat rotted on the sodden fields. Many died of starvation. Elizabeth opened her granaries on the Wartburg, and gave corn to the poor, and sent it to those too weak to climb the steep rock. She built a hospital at the foot of the castle rock, in which she nursed twenty-eight sick folk. The steward and others complained that she was wasting the stores. They told her husband what she had done. One day, says the legend, as he was walking up the steep path to the castle, he met Elizabeth with her lap full of loaves. “What have you there?” he asked; and, drawing her mantle away, he saw that her lap was full of roses. Looking up, he beheld a cross shining in the air over her head.

## Evening Prayer

Pope Honorius III had urged Christendom to make another effort to recover Jerusalem. Emperor Frederick II was to lead the crusade. It was preached throughout Germany; the most liberal promises were made to those who joined. Honorius died in 1227, and Gregory IX succeeded him as the crusaders assembled. Ludwig, the Landgrave of Thuringia, was persuaded by the Bishop of Hildesheim to take the cross. As he left Elizabeth, he showed her his ring. “Dear sister,” as he was accustomed to call her, “look at this signet ring with the Lamb of God engraved on the stone. Should this be brought to you, it will be a token of my death. Then God bless you and the fruit of your womb. Then forget me not, sweet sister, in your prayers to God.” The crusaders set off and encountered extreme heat and sickness. Ludwig became gravely ill at Otranto. He lay in his bed with death before his eyes. Before he died he removed his ring, and gave it to a trusty knight to bear to Thuringia and give to his mother, without mentioning to anyone else what had befallen him, so that the news might not reach Elizabeth suddenly, but that his mother might be able gently to break it to her. His last thoughts were on her.

No sooner had the Landgrave Henry heard of the death of his brother than he determined to seize the government of the principality for himself. Henry drove Elizabeth from the Wartburg without compunction, without allowing her even money to maintain her position. She went sorrowfully forth with her three little children, followed by some of her faithful attendants. The abbess of Kitzingen sent two waggons to fetch the landgravine, her children, and faithful attendants, and they were conducted out of Thuringia into Franconia. The Bishop of Bamberg then sent for the landgravine. He offered her his castle of Pottenstein, and to maintain her as befitted her rank, till some arrangement could be arrived at for the future. The offer was too kind and considerate to be refused. She left one of her little daughters, Sophia, with the abbess of Kitzingen, to be there brought up for the monastic life, and departed with her little son and her other daughter for Pottenstein. The bishop was anxious to get her married again to some powerful noble or prince, who could maintain the claim of her little boy against his uncles. But Elizabeth shrank from the thoughts of a second marriage. “I promised,” she said, “that I would never belong to anyone but Ludwig. I made the promise to God and to my lord when he was alive, and I cannot fail my word.”

After a sharp rebuke by Rudolf of Vargila—the son of Walther, who had brought Elizabeth in her silver cradle from Hungary to Thuringia—Henry and Conrad were obliged to ask pardon of Elizabeth, and promise to treat her better for the future. They agreed to take her back to Wartburg and grant her the town of Marburg. Elizabeth experienced great discomfort at Marburg. To what this was due does not transpire. The citizens certainly resented her presence among them, but whether this was due to her mode of living, or to the influence of the landgrave who fomented hostility against her, does not appear certain. She was forced to leave Marburg and take refuge in a little village, where she found a poor, dilapidated building in which to take shelter. There she remained till a little timber and plaster house had been erected for her outside Marburg. She then had a large hospital attached to it in which she could nurse the sick. She sold all that remained to her of jewels and silks which had been brought with her from Hungary to provide for the support of her sick people. She took into her hospital the most helpless and neglected cases, and attended to their necessities with the utmost gentleness and patience. The sick children especially demanded and obtained her tenderest solicitude. When not attending to the sick she occupied herself with spinning, and sold what she had spun for the benefit of the hospital.

When Elizabeth was ill and laid up in her bed, which was often the case, her busy hands were still occupied with spinning; and when that was taken from her, she occupied her fingers in pulling out the hemp for future use. Her health was declining; she needed nourishing food, tender nursing, and gentle treatment. However, Conrad of Marburg, the inquisitor who became her spiritual director, made her fast with redoubled rigour, became more brutal in his treatment of her, and removed from her Ysentrude and Jutta, her two faithful attendants, ladies by birth, who had been brought up with her from childhood, and whom she loved as sisters. At last her health, which had long been failing under this usage, gave way. On the night of her death, about midnight, she meditated a great deal on the words of Holy Scripture, and on sermons she had heard. When midnight approached, she said, “Now comes the hour when the [Mother Maid Mary](/people/saints/biblical/the-blessed-virgin-mary) brought the child Jesus into the world, and the star appeared in the East to guide the Wise Men to his cradle. He came to redeem the world, and he will redeem me. And now is the time when he rose from the grave and broke the prison doors of hell, to release the imprisoned souls, and he will now release me.” Elizabeth died on November 17, 1231, when only twenty-four years old, and was buried in the hospital church four days after.[^1]

<Initial text="Saint Francis of Assisi" />

**October 4**<br/>
(1226 AD)<br/>
*Commemoration*

## Morning Prayer

[St. Francis](/people/saints/medieval/francis-of-assisi) was born in Assisi in 1182, of good, though hardly noble, parents. His father, Pietro di Bernardone, was a merchant; his mother's name was Pica. At his baptism, the child was called by his mother John. At the time his father was absent in France. On his return, his name was changed familiarly to Francis. Pietro, who destined his son to succeed him in his business, took pains to have the child instructed in French. The young folks of Assisi, unable to appreciate Pietro's reasoning, ridiculed the boy for speaking French, and called him, in jest, *Franciscus* or "Frenchman". Francis, in his youth, was keen in the pursuit of money, but no miser. He spent freely but not extravagantly, dressed handsomely, and ate and drank of the best. He was fond of fun and good company, but he never seems to have stained his youth with sins of unchastity, nor to have been immodest in his conversation.

About the year 1201, the city of Perugia was at war with Assisi, and in one of the frays between the rival citizens, Francis was captured and detained a year in prison in Perugia. On his return to Assisi, his love of the sober routine of a merchant's life gave way to a taste for arms. He had a friend who proposed to go to Apulia to support Walter III of Brienne in his campaign in the kingdom of Sicily. Whilst the project was shaping in his mind, he met "a certain soldier of honour and courage, but poor and vilely clad." The feeling of brotherhood towards him moved him to generosity. He took off his fine clothes and gave them to the poor old warrior. This kind act was rewarded on the next night by a remarkable dream. He beheld a magnificent palace with an armoury filled with every variety of weapon, each signed with the cross; and flags and symbols of military triumph were hung along the walls. "All these," said a voice, "are for thee and for thy soldiers." Little did he then imagine what this dream signified, and that the weapons of his warfare were not to be carnal.

Francis then set out on his way to Apulia. He got as far as Spoleto, but there fell ill. While he thus lay one night in the feverish succession of heat and cold, half asleep, half stupefied, he suddenly heard a voice which questioned him: "Francis, whom does it profit most to follow, the master or the servant?" "The master," answered the sick man, promptly. "Why then," asked the voice, "do you leave the master for the servant, the prince for his subject?" The young Francis said, like Saul, "Lord, what wilt thou have me to do?" "Return to thy country," answered the voice, "and then it will be told thee what thou must do." The next morning the fever left him, and he remounted his horse, not to go on to Apulia, but to return to Assisi. But it was not to the joyous, careless life of former days. The current of his life gradually and imperceptibly swept into the new channel of piety—not of active charity only, but of deep meditation on God and the mystery of redemption.

Back at Assisi, the church of San Damiano had fallen into disrepair. The ruinous condition of the sacred building forced itself on his notice, and then suddenly flamed up in him the resolve to restore the dilapidated sanctuary. He hastened home, carried off several bales of cloth from his father's warehouse (to which he had no right), conveyed them to Foligno, and there sold them, together with the horse which had brought them thither. Then he ran with the money to the church of San Damiano, and offered it to the priest who ministered there. This worthy man, surprised at the large sum offered and the excitement of the young man, before receiving it asked questions about how he had come by it, and elicited the facts. The bishop exhorted Francis to return the money to his father. "Not only the money," said Francis, "but everything that can be called his, even the clothes he gave me, I will restore." Then he began the work which lay near his heart. He brought stones from the quarries, hewed them, shaped them, and built them into the walls of San Damiano. The townsfolk looked on in wonder, and gradually lent their assistance; and so he succeeded in restoring the church. When the church of San Damiano was finished, Francis restored two others: a church of [St. Peter](/people/saints/apostles/peter) and that of Santa Maria degli Angeli at the Portiuncula, which became from that time his home.

## Evening Prayer

In 1208, while St. Francis was hearing Mass one day in the little church he so loved, something in the Gospel struck him as it had never done before: "Provide neither gold, nor silver, nor brass in your purses, nor scrip for your journey, neither two coats, neither shoes, nor yet staves (Matt. 10:9-10). And as ye go, preach, saying, 'The kingdom of heaven is at hand'" (Matt. 10:7). Leaving the church, he divested himself of his shoes, cast away his staff, loosed the leather girdle from his waist, and supplied its place by a piece of cord, the first thing that was ready at hand. He began to preach, each time beginning with the salutation, "The peace of God be with you;" and he was heard by all. Then others began to join him, and St. Francis sent them out to preach by twos, as our Lord had sent his disciples. And when the little house which they inhabited at the Portiuncula was so full that there was scarcely room for them all to lie down in it, it became necessary to give to the family a constitution. So the *Rule* was drawn up, consisting, like the other monastic rules, of the three great vows of poverty, chastity, and obedience, differing only in so far that the poverty ordained by Francis was absolute. Day by day they were to live by God's providence, eating what they were given in charity, taking no thought as to how they were to be fed or wherewithal clothed. And they were to preach. This was their special work. They were to be the heralds of God to the world, to proclaim the coming of his kingdom.

The years between 1214 and 1219 abound in beautiful stories of his tenderness, kindness, humanity, and of the beautiful courtesy of his character. He was a man overflowing with sympathy for man and beast, for all God's creatures, wherever and howsoever he encountered them. Not only was every man his brother, but every animal: the sheep in the fields, the birds in the branches, the donkey on which he rode, the bees which took refuge in his kind protection. He was the friend of everything that suffered or rejoiced; no emotion went beyond his sympathy; his heart rose to see the gladness of nature, and melted over the distresses of the smallest and meanest creature on the face of the earth. Having obtained the formal ratification of the *Rule* in a papal bull dated November 29, 1223, Francis made another request to the pope, of a different character. He asked to be allowed to introduce into the Franciscan churches representations of the manger of Bethlehem at Christmas, so as to capture the popular imagination and make an impression upon the unlearned. He obtained the requisite permission, and then, going to Greccio, a little place not far from Assisi, he had a stable with manger and straw erected in the church; every arrangement was made, when the solemn Christmas night arrived, for giving to the people a visible representation of the occurrences of the night at Bethlehem.

St. Francis had reached the age of forty-two when a mysterious event occurred in his life, which marked him out among the other saints of the calendar in a special and extraordinary manner. A certain noble, Orlando of Chiusi, gave to St. Francis a rocky height, Mount La Verna, on his lands, as a kind of hermitage and place of retreat. On Holy Cross Day, whilst Francis prayed, there appeared over him a great figure as of a seraph. This solemn and wonderful apparition had the arms extended and feet conjoined, as if fixed to a cross. It had six wings, two of which were elevated over the head, two extended as if for flight, and the other two veiling the entire body. "And when this vision disappeared, a wonderful ardour remained in his soul; and in his flesh still more marvellously appeared the <Definition lang="greek" anchor="stigmata" text="stigmata" /> of the Lord Jesus Christ, which the man of God carried concealed to his death, not willing to publish the secret of God."

After the return of St. Francis to his convent, he concentrated his failing powers on the continuance of his work. The pitcher was broken, and the light streamed through at every crevice. He summoned to him all the brethren, and gave them his dying advice and blessing. When he had said all he had to say, he commanded the Gospels to be brought to him, and the passage to be read beginning, "Before the Feast of the Passover," the commencement of the 13th chapter of [St. John](/people/saints/apostles/john). When the reading was ended, he began, with broken voice, to sing, "I cried unto the Lord with my voice; yea, even unto the Lord did I make my supplication" (Ps. 142:1). Such was the end of the life of St. Francis of Assisi, a life filled with one great master thought, which dominated all other motives of humanity and impulses of nature: the desire to be like Christ. He died on Saturday, October 3, and he was buried in the Church of San Giorgio in Assisi on the following day.[^1]

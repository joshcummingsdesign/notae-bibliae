<Initial text="Saint Luke" />

**October 18**<br/>
Evangelist (1st century AD)<br/>
*Feast*

## Morning Prayer

The name of [Luke](/people/saints/evangelists/luke) occurs only thrice in the New Testament (Col. 4:14; 2 Tim. 4:11; Philem. 1:24). Most probably in all three cases the third evangelist is the person spoken of. Combining the traditional element with the scriptural, we are able to trace the following dim outlines of the Evangelist's life. He was born at Antioch, in Syria, in what condition of life is uncertain; but it has been thought, from the form of his name, that he was an emancipated slave. Luke is an abbreviated form of Lucanus. That he was taught the science of medicine does not prove that he was of higher birth than the rest of the disciples, as many great men had one of their slaves instructed in medicine, so as to serve as family physician. A later tradition holds that St. Luke was a painter. [St. Irenaeus](/people/saints/greek-fathers/irenaeus-of-lyon), [Eusebius](/people/early-christian-writers/eusebius), and [St. Jerome](/people/saints/latin-fathers/jerome-of-stridon) confidently assert that he was a physician. [St. Paul](/people/saints/apostles/paul) speaks of "Luke, the beloved physician" (Col. 4:14). St. Jerome says that he was more acquainted with Greek letters than with Hebrew. He was not born a Jew, for he is not reckoned among them "of the circumcision" by St. Paul (cf. Col. 4:11). The date of his conversion is uncertain. [Epiphanius](/people/saints/early/epiphanius-of-salamis) and others state that he was one of the seventy disciples. The Greek Menology asserts that he was one of the two who journeyed to Emmaus with the risen Redeemer.

The first ray of historical light falls on the Evangelist when he joins St. Paul at Troas, and shares his journey into Macedonia. The sudden transition to the first person plural in Acts 16:10, is most naturally explained by supposing that Luke, the writer of the Acts, formed one of St. Paul's company from this point. As far as Philippi the Evangelist journeyed with the Apostle. The resumption of the third person on St. Paul's departure from that place (Acts 17:1) would show that St. Luke was now left behind. During the rest of St. Paul's second missionary journey we hear no more of him; but on the third journey, the same indication reminds us that Luke is again in the company (Acts 20:5), having joined it at Philippi, where he had been left. With the Apostle he passed through Miletus, Tyre, and Caesarea, to Jerusalem. Between the two visits of Paul to Philippi, seven years had elapsed (51–58 AD), which the Evangelist may have spent in Philippi and its neighbourhood, preaching the Gospel.

There remains one passage, which, if it refers to St. Luke, must belong to this period: "We have sent with him," that is, [Titus](/people/saints/biblical/titus), "the brother whose praise is in the Gospel throughout all the churches" (2 Cor. 8:18). The subscription of the epistle sets forth that it was "written from Philippi, a city of Macedonia, by Titus and Lucas," and it is probable that this Luke was the companion of Titus, and, therefore, the brother whose praise was in all the churches. If this be so, we are to suppose that during the three months' sojourn of Paul at Philippi, Luke was sent from that place to Corinth on this errand. He again appears in the company of St. Paul in his journey to Rome (Acts 27:1). He remained at his side during his first imprisonment (Col. 4:14); and if it be supposed that the Second Epistle to Timothy was written during the second imprisonment, then the testimony of that epistle shows that he continued faithful to the Apostle to the end of his afflictions (2 Tim. 4:11).

After the death of St. Paul, the acts of his faithful companion are hopelessly obscure to us. The Greek Menaea says that he lived to the age of eighty; St. Epiphanius that he preached in Dalmatia, Gaul, Italy, and Macedonia. [St. Gregory of Nazianzus](/people/saints/cappadocian-fathers/gregory-of-nazianzus) makes Achaia the scene of his preaching. A passage in his writings seems to imply that Luke was a martyr, as he classes him with [James](/people/saints/apostles/james), [Stephen](/people/saints/biblical/stephen), and [Andrew](/people/saints/apostles/andrew), as those who had exposed themselves to suffering for Christ; but he may only mean that he endured much, not that he shed his blood in martyrdom. And Elias of Crete, the commentator on St. Gregory of Nazianzus, denies that Luke was a martyr. Gaudentius, Bishop of Brescia, in the 5th century, speaks of Luke as a martyr, and says that he suffered at Patras. St. Luke is represented with the ox (Rev. 4:7), or painting the portrait of the [Blessed Virgin](/people/saints/biblical/the-blessed-virgin-mary).[^1]

## Evening Prayer

*<Red text="✠" /> From the writings of [St. Ambrose](/people/saints/latin-fathers/ambrose-of-milan):* There are three things which the philosophers of this world have thought most excellent, namely that wisdom is threefold, being either natural, or moral, or rational. Truly, there is natural wisdom in the [Book of the Evangelist John](/bible/new-testament/gospels/john). For no one, I dare say, has seen the majesty of God with such sublimity of wisdom, and preserved it for us in his own words. He transcends the clouds, he transcends the virtues of the heavens, he transcends the angels, and finds the Word in the beginning and sees the Word with God (John 1:1). But who has more morally, according to man, followed each particular than [St. Matthew](/people/saints/apostles/matthew), who gave us precepts for living? What is more rational than that admirable coupling which [St. Mark](/people/saints/evangelists/mark) thought should be placed right at the beginning? "Behold," he says, "I send my angel" (Mark 1:2); and, "The voice of one crying in the desert" (Mark 1:3).

But St. Luke kept, as it were, a certain historical order, and revealed to us more of the miracles of the Lord's deeds, yet in such a way that the history of this Gospel embraced the virtues of all wisdom. For what is more excellent for natural wisdom than that he disclosed the Holy Spirit to have been the Creator even of the Lord's incarnation? (Luke 1:35). And moral things in the same book, when in the beatitudes he teaches: how I ought to love an enemy (Luke 6:27); how not to strike back, and not to return blows to one who strikes (Luke 6:29); how to do good, to lend without hope of recovery, or of remuneration and reward (Luke 6:35). For reward more easily follows one who does not expect it. He also teaches rational things, when we read that "he who is faithful in the least, is faithful also in the greater" (Luke 16:10). What further shall I say of natural things, in that he teaches that the Lord alone is the only-begotten Son of God, in whose Passion darkness came over the day, the earth was darkened, the sun fled? (Luke 23:44–45).

When, therefore, we read these things, let us consider them carefully, that they may be able to shine forth better for us in their proper places. For "he who seeks finds; and to him who knocks it shall be opened" (Luke 11:10). Diligence opens for itself the door of truth, and therefore let us obey the heavenly precepts. For it was not said idly to man, what was not said to any of the other living creatures: "In the sweat of thy face shalt thou eat thy bread" (Gen. 3:19). For to those animals which are irrational by nature, the earth has been commanded by God's order to furnish food; but to man alone, that he might exercise the rational faculty he has received, the course of life is prescribed in labour.

For we said that this book of the Gospel was composed in an historical style. Indeed, we see a fuller effort applied, by comparison with the others, to the description of events rather than the expression of precepts. And the Evangelist himself, in the historical manner, took his beginning from narration: "There was," he says, "in the days of Herod king of Judaea a certain priest named Zechariah" (Luke 1:5), and he follows out that history with full arrangement. Whence also those who thought the four forms of animals which are revealed in the Book of Revelation should be understood as the four books of the Gospel (Rev. 4:7), wish this book to be figured under the likeness of the ox; for the ox is the sacrificial victim. And well does this book of the Gospel fit the ox, because it began with priests, and ended in the ox, who, taking up the sins of all, was offered for the life of the whole world; for that ox is priestly. Indeed, the same is both ox and priest: priest, because he is our propitiator, for we have him as advocate with the Father; ox, because by his own blood he has washed and redeemed us.[^2]

The feast of [The Conception of the Blessed Virgin Mary](/liturgy/liturgical-year/saints/conception-of-the-blessed-virgin-mary) has been long observed in the month of December—nine months before the feast of [Our Lady's Nativity](/liturgy/liturgical-year/saints/nativity-of-the-blessed-virgin-mary). The theology of [Mary's](/people/saints/biblical/blessed-virgin-mary) conception, however, has been the subject of debate over the centuries, particularly concerning her relationship to [original sin](/meditations/original-sin). How could the Mother of God bear the sinless Son of God if she herself had been conceived in sin? The Eastern Church, which does not affirm the doctrine of original sin, teaches that Mary, like everyone else, was subject to mortality and temptation, yet committed no personal sins.[^1] In the West, however, the doctrine of original sin gave rise to differing views concerning Mary's sanctification.

## Thomas Aquinas

[St. Thomas Aquinas](/people/saints/medieval/thomas-aquinas) argued that Mary was indeed conceived in original sin, but was sanctified in her mother's womb.

> "It is reasonable to believe that she who brought forth the only-begotten of the Father, full of grace and truth, received greater privileges of grace than all others. Hence, we read that the angel addressed her in the words, 'Hail full of grace!' (Luke 1:28). Moreover, it is to be observed that it was granted by way of privilege to others to be sanctified in the womb. For instance, to Jeremiah, to whom it was said, 'Before thou camest forth out of the womb, I sanctified thee' (Jer. 1:5); and again, to John the Baptist, of whom it is written, 'He shall be filled with the Holy Spirit even from his mother's womb' (Luke 1:15). It is therefore with reason that we believe the Blessed Virgin to have been sanctified before her birth from the womb."
>
> <cite>[Aquinas, ST III, q. 27, a. 1, co.](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.III.Q27.A1.C)</cite>

He further clarifies that this sanctification occurred after the infusion of her soul.

> "The sanctification of the Blessed Virgin cannot be understood as having taken place before animation, for two reasons. First, ... sin cannot be taken away except by grace, the subject of which is the rational creature alone. Therefore, before the infusion of the rational soul, the Blessed Virgin was not sanctified.
>
> Second, ... [if she were] sanctified before animation, she could never have incurred the stain of original sin, and thus she would not have needed redemption and salvation, which is by Christ, of whom it is written, 'He shall save his people from their sins' (Matt. 1:21). But this is unfitting, implying that Christ is not the Savior of all men, as he is called (1 Tim 4:10). It remains, therefore, that the Blessed Virgin was sanctified after animation."
>
> <cite>[Aquinas, ST III, q. 28, a. 1, co.](https://aquinas.cc/la/en/~ST.III.Q27.A2.C)</cite>


## Duns Scotus

[Duns Scotus](/people/medieval-theologians/duns-scotus) held that God could indeed have sanctified Mary at any point in time, yet considered it most fitting to attribute to her preservation from original sin from the first moment of her existence.

> "God could have brought it about that Mary was never in original sin. He could also have brought it about that she was in original sin for only a single instant. He could also have brought it about that she remained in original sin for a period of time, and that she was cleansed in the final moment of that period. ... But as to which of these three possibilities occurred, all of which have been shown to be possible, God alone knows. But if it be not contrary to the authority of the Church or the authority of Scripture, it seems probable that we should attribute to Mary that which is the more excellent. ... Mary stood in the greatest need of Christ as her Redeemer. For she would indeed have inherited original sin by reason of the common law of human propagation had she not been prevented by the grace of the Mediator. And just as others needed Christ, so that through his merits, the sin they had already inherited might be forgiven them, so she had a greater need of a Mediator who would prevent her from ever inheriting sin."
>
> <cite>[Scotus, Ordinatio III, d. 3, q. 1](https://archive.org/details/operaomniaiussue0009duns/page/168/mode/2up)</cite>

In 1854, the Roman Catholic Church formally adopted the doctrine advanced by Duns Scotus, declaring that Mary was preserved from original sin "in the first instance of her conception." Following this declaration, the word "Immaculate" was officially added to the name of the feast on the Roman Calendar.[^2]

## Martin Luther

Martin Luther in his 1527 *Sermon on the Conception of Mary*, expounds a view similar to that of Thomas Aquinas, explaining that Mary was conceived in original sin, yet sanctified at the moment of ensoulment. Although he notes that the doctrine is not found in Scripture, he nevertheless addresses it because the feast had become the occasion of much controversy and dispute.

> "This feast has produced much displeasure, quarrelling, and strife among the monks, without any benefit or profit, since not a single letter concerning it is found in the Gospel or elsewhere in Scripture. From this we ought also to see how the devil has blinded and led us astray, so that we have devoted ourselves most of all to that which has neither been commanded to us nor is necessary for us to know, while that which has been commanded to us we have neglected. So it has always been, and so it still is, and indeed so it will continue as long as the world stands. Therefore, let no one be offended by it, for the world remains the world, do what one will. Today much has been said about original sin. If only they had gotten it right! Therefore we too must say a little about it."
>
> <cite>[Luther, WA 17/2:280](https://www.maartenluther.com/17-II_werkekritischege17luthuoft.pdf)</cite>

After discussing the primacy of the Word of God and introducing the doctrine of original sin, Luther turns to the conception of Mary.

> "Since the Virgin Mary was naturally born of father and mother, many have held that she too was conceived in original sin. Yet all of them agree that she was sanctified in her mother's womb, and that her parents conceived her without lust and desire. But some, wishing to settle the matter, have said that there are two kinds of human conception. One which proceeds from the natural union of man and woman, and another which takes place when the body has been formed in the mother's womb and when the soul is infused by God the Creator.
>
> We are not speaking here of the first conception. Nor does it matter much whether the Virgin Mary was conceived according to the common manner of all mankind, provided that Christ alone be excepted in this respect, having been conceived without the involvement of man. This had to be so, for Christ was to be conceived as God and man, perfect in all his members; therefore it was necessary that his conception should be the most spiritual and the holiest of all.
>
> But in the conception of the Virgin Mary—whose body was formed in due course according to the customary manner of other children until the infusion of the soul—it was not necessary that her conception be like his, for she was preserved from original sin with respect to the soul. This conception, namely, the infusion of the soul, is piously and devoutly believed to have been free from original sin, so that she was at the same time cleansed from original sin and adorned with God's gifts to receive a holy soul infused into her. And thus, from the very first moment she began to live, she was free from all sin."
>
> <cite>[Luther, WA 17/2:287–288](https://www.maartenluther.com/17-II_werkekritischege17luthuoft.pdf)</cite>

## John Calvin

[John Calvin](/meditations/a-brief-history-of-the-reformation-part-3#john-calvin) rejected the idea that Mary was preserved from original sin. His comments on Canon 23 of the Council of Trent provide insight into his position.

> "As to the special privilege of the Virgin Mary, when they produce the celestial diploma we shall believe what they say; for to what do they here give the name of the Church, but just to the Council of Clermont? [Augustine](/people/saints/latin-fathers/augustine-of-hippo) was certainly a member of the Church, and though he in one passage chooses, in order to avoid obloquy, rather to be silent respecting the Blessed Virgin, he uniformly, without making her an exception, describes the whole race of Adam as involved in sin. Nay, he even almost in distinct terms classes her among sinners: when writing to Marcellinus, he says, "They err greatly who hold that any of the saints except Christ require not to use this prayer: 'Forgive us our debts.' In so doing, they by no means please the saints whom they laud." [Chrysostom](/people/saints/greek-fathers/john-chrysostom) and [Ambrose](/people/saints/latin-fathers/ambrose-of-milan), who suspect her of having been tempted by ambition, were members of the Church."
>
> <cite>[Calvin, "Sixth Session: Canon XXIII", in *Tracts Containing Antidote to the Council of Trent*](https://archive.org/details/tractsrelatingto3calv/page/156/mode/2up)</cite>

## Conclusion

Despite these differing views, one thing remains certain: Mary truly is blessed among women, for God bestowed upon her such grace that she was made ready to bear the Son of God, the Savior of the world. Therefore, on this day, let all honor and reverence be given her, Mary the Mother of God.

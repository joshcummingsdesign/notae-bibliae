## Restored Classical Pronunciation

Restored Classical Pronunciation is a modern reconstruction of the phonology of Latin as spoken by well-educated members of ancient Roman society of the late Republic and early Empire (c. 1st century BC – 1st century AD). Though a modern reconstruction, it is a good baseline against which we can compare other systems of Latin pronunciation. Its key features include the following:

- **a, e, i, o, u, y** sound as they do in the International Phonetic Alphabet (IPA).
- **u** as a consonant (also written as **v**) sounds like “w”.
- **c** is always hard.
- **g** is always hard.
- **ph, th, ch** are not fricative; the **h** adds only a puff of air.
- **r** is trilled.

## Developments in Antiquity

While Restored Classical Pronunciation serves as a good baseline, it is important to note that developments in pronunciation were taking place as early as the 1st and 2nd centuries AD. These developments include the following:

- **ae/oe** sound like **e** in rural areas.
- **v** develops a fricative sound.
- **ti** before another vowel develops the “tsi” sound.
- **ph** softens to an “f” sound.[^1]

## The Middle Ages

By the Middle Ages, Latin pronunciation varied by region. The features of Spanish Latin, for example, include the following:

- **y** sounds like **i**.
- **c** before the **i** and **e** sounds become “tsi” and “tse”, respectively.
- **ch** before a vowel sounds like “ch”, as in “cherubim”.
- **h** is is otherwise silent.

## Ecclesiastical Pronunciation

By the 20th century, the influence of secular music on the Church had contributed to a departure from Gregorian chant. In 1903, Pope Pius X promoted its restoration, and encouraged Italian Latin pronunciation, which became the standard in the Roman Catholic Church. Today, this is what most people think of when they hear the term “Ecclesiastical Latin”. Its key features include the following:

- **c** before the **i** and **e** sounds become “chi” and “che”, respectively.
- **g** before the **i** and **e** sounds become “ji” and “je”, respectively.
- **gn** makes the Spanish “ñ” sound.
- ***mihi*** and ***nihil*** famously become “miki” and “nikil”.[^2]

## Which One to Use

Choosing a pronunciation system depends on one's particular purpose. Recreating the sound of a well-educated, urban Roman citizen in the 1st century may lead one to Restored Classical Pronunciation. Singing in a Roman Catholic choir may lead another to Ecclesiastical Pronunciation. Yet another may find it beautiful and fitting to imagine recreating the sound of [St. Dominic](/people/saints/medieval/dominic-of-guzman) chanting at the Cathedral of Osma in Spain. Latin pronunciation has a rich and complex history, and one can appreciate both the modern research into its ancient sounds and its natural development into the Romance languages we know and love today.

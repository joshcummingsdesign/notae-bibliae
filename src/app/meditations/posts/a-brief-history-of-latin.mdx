**1st Century BC** — Cicero has an immense influence on Latin prose, rhetoric, philosophy, and political thought. His works shape the development of Latin as a literary language. Latin from this period becomes known as "Classical Latin".

**4th Century** — [St. Ambrose](/people/saints/latin-fathers/ambrose-of-milan) introduces Latin plainchant into the Western Church.[^1]

**5th Century** — [St. Jerome](/people/saints/latin-fathers/jerome-of-stridon) finishes his translation of the [Vulgate](/bible/translations/vulgate). A *<Definition lang="latin" anchor="schola-cantorum" text="schola cantorum" />* is founded in Rome.[^2] By the fall of the Western Roman Empire, Latin begins to take on different pronunciations in different regions.

**6th Century** — [St. Gregory the Great](/people/saints/latin-fathers/gregory-the-great) collects and organizes Latin plainchant, and revitalizes the *Schola Cantorum* in Rome. Thus, Latin plainchant becomes known as "Gregorian chant".[^3]

**9th Century** — Charlemagne enlists Alcuin of York to reform education and promote more consistent Latin spelling, grammar, and pronunciation.[^4]

**14th Century** — The regional variants of Latin are written down phonetically and eventually evolve into what become the Romance Languages: Spanish, Portuguese, French, Italian, and Romanian. Humanists like Francis Petrarch spark a renewed interest in the study of Classical Latin during the Renaissance.[^5]

**16th Century** — Erasmus revives the study of Classical Latin pronunciation.

**19th Century** — The study and reconstruction of Classical Latin pronunciation continues and becomes what is often called "Restored Classical Pronunciation".

**20th Century** — The influence of secular music on the Church had contributed to a departure from Gregorian chant. In 1903, Pope Pius X promoted its restoration, and encouraged Italian Latin pronunciation, which became the standard in the Roman Catholic Church. Today, this is what most people think of when they hear the term "Ecclesiastical Latin".[^6]

The legend of the child who told [St. Augustine](/people/saints/latin-fathers/augustine-of-hippo) that one could sooner empty the ocean with a shell than fully comprehend the mystery of the Trinity has long appeared in Christian art and literature. In the early 14th century, an apocryphal letter attributed to St. Augustine drew a striking parallel to the legend as we know it today.

> "For on that very day and hour when the most holy [Jerome](/people/saints/latin-fathers/jerome-of-stridon), having been stripped of the garment of corruptible flesh, put on the raiment of perpetual immortality and of inestimable joy and glory, I was resting in my cell at Hippo. I eagerly pondered the measure of glory and joy possessed by the souls of the blessed who rejoice with Christ. ... Suddenly an ineffable light—such as had never been seen in our time and could scarcely be described in words—together with an ineffable and unheard-of fragrance of every perfume, entered the cell in which I stood at the hour of Compline. ... As my thoughts clamoured within me as to what this might be, a voice issued from the light, speaking these words: 'Augustine, what seekest thou? Dost thou think that thou canst place the whole sea within a little vessel, enclose the entire earth within a little hand, or fix the heavens so that they cease from their accustomed motions? Shall thine eye see what no human eye has been able to see? Shall thine ear hear what no ear has received through sound? Dost thou think thyself able to understand what the human heart has neither understood nor even conceived? What limit can there be to that which is infinite? By what measure wilt thou measure that which is immeasurable? The whole sea might sooner be enclosed within the narrowest vessel, the entire earth held within a little hand, and the heavens cease from their continual motion, than thou shouldest understand even the smallest portion of the joy and glory which the souls of the blessed enjoy without end, unless thou, as I, wert taught by experience. Run yet the brief remaining course of time; attempt not the impossible until the course of thy life is fulfilled. Seek not here those things which can be found only in that place whither I so happily hasten. Strive here to perform such deeds that hereafter thou mayest fully and eternally possess there what thou now desirest to understand in some measure; for those who enter that place never depart.'"
>
> <cite>[Pseudo-Augustine, "On the Magnificence of St. Jerome", PL 22:284](https://archive.org/details/bim_early-english-books-1641-1700_patrologiae-cursus-completus-_1845_22/page/n189/mode/2up)</cite>

Later in the 14th century, Italian bishop Petrus de Natalibus recorded the familiar form of the legend in his *Catalogue of the Saints*. [The Bollandists](/glossary/historical-terms#bollandists) cite this in their 18th-century *Acts of the Saints*.

> "While considering the composition of his book *On the Trinity*, Augustine passed along the shore and saw a child who had made a little hole upon the beach and was pouring into it water drawn from the sea with a shell. When Augustine asked the child what he was doing, the child replied that he intended to empty the sea with the shell and pour it into that hole. When Augustine said that this was impossible and laughed at the child's simplicity, the child told him that it would be easier for him to accomplish this than for Augustine to explain in his book even the smallest part of the mysteries of the Trinity. He compared the hole to the book, the sea to the Trinity, and the shell to Augustine's understanding. Having said this, the child disappeared. Augustine was thereby humbled and, having first offered prayer, composed his book *On the Trinity* as best he could."
>
> <cite>["On St. Augustine", *Acts of the Saints*, p. 357](https://archive.org/details/actasanctorum40unse/page/n399/mode/2up)</cite>

In the 15th century, William Caxton, who introduced the printing press into England, writes in his *Golden Legend* that although he could not find the account in any of his English, French, or Latin exemplars, he had seen it painted upon an altar dedicated to St. Augustine at St. Paul's Church in Antwerp. In Caxton's account, the child uses a spoon rather than a shell.

> "As [Agustine] went by the seaside in Africa [contemplating] the Trinity, he found ... a little child which had made a little pit in the sand, and in his hand a little spoon. And with the spoon he took out water of the large sea and poured it into the pit. And when St. Augustine beheld him he marvelled, and [asked him what he was doing]. And he answered and said, 'I will lade out and bring all this water of the sea into this pit.' 'What?' said [Augustine], 'It is impossible! How may it be done? The sea is so great and large, and thy pit and spoon so little.' '[To tell you the truth]', said [the child], 'I shall lightlier and sooner draw all the water of the sea and bring it into this pit than thou shalt bring the mystery of the Trinity and his divinity into thy little understanding ...; for the mystery of the Trinity is greater and larger to the comparison of thy wit and brain than is this great sea unto this little pit.' And therewith the child vanished away."
>
> <cite>["The Life of St. Augustine", *The Golden Legend*, pp. 65–66](https://archive.org/details/goldenlegendorli05jaco/page/64/mode/2up)</cite>

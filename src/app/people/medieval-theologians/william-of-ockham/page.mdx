<Initial text="William of Ockham" />

[William of Ockham](/glossary/names#william) was born around 1287 in Ockham, Surrey, and entered the [Franciscan Order](/people/saints/medieval/francis-of-assisi) at an early age. After receiving his initial education in London, he studied theology at the University of Oxford, where he established himself as one of the leading scholastic philosophers of his generation.

Ockham sought to simplify philosophy and theology by eliminating unnecessary complexity, an approach that became known as "Ockham's razor". Although influenced by [Duns Scotus](/people/medieval-theologians/duns-scotus), he developed his own distinctive views on philosophy and theology, defending nominalism (the view that universals and abstract objects do not actually exist other than being merely names or labels) and emphasizing that faith is necessary for grasping theological truths, which go beyond what reason can comprehend.

Like many medieval theologians, Ockham wrote a commentary on [Peter Lombard's](/people/medieval-theologians/peter-lombard) *Sentences*. Some of its theological opinions were questioned, leading to his summons before the papal court at Avignon. At the same time, he became deeply involved in the Franciscan controversy over apostolic poverty. After Pope John XXII condemned the Franciscan position, Ockham fled Avignon in 1328, took refuge with Emperor Louis IV of Bavaria, and was subsequently excommunicated for leaving without papal permission.

Ockham spent the remainder of his life writing on theology, philosophy, and the relationship between spiritual and temporal authority. His contributions to logic, metaphysics, epistemology, ethics, political philosophy, and theology established him as one of the most influential thinkers of the late Middle Ages, alongside [St. Thomas Aquinas](/people/saints/medieval/thomas-aquinas) and Duns Scotus.

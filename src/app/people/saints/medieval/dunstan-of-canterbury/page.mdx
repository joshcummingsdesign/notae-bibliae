**Bishop** (988 AD)

[St. Dunstan](/glossary/names#dunstan) was born near Glastonbury in around 909 AD, after regular monastic life had largely declined in England. Many monasteries had been destroyed during the Scandinavian invasions, while others had fallen under lay control or become communities of clerics without a full monastic rule. Glastonbury, where Dunstan was educated in his youth by Irish monks, had scarcely survived. There, the young Dunstan is said to have received a vision of the abbey restored to its former splendor.

Gifted in learning, music, manuscript illumination, and metalworking, Dunstan spent time at King Athelstan’s court, where he gained firsthand experience of England’s political life. The jealousy of his fellow courtiers, however, led to his dismissal. Around this time, he also suffered a serious illness, which deepened his sense of spiritual calling and turned his attention more fully toward religious life. Under the influence of his kinsman Alphege, Bishop of Winchester, Dunstan eventually withdrew to a small cell near Glastonbury, embracing a life of prayer, fasting, study, and manual labor. When King Edmund succeeded Athelstan, he recalled Dunstan to court, but Dunstan’s uncompromising virtue again brought him into conflict with the king and courtiers, and he was forced to leave. While hunting on the Cheddar Hills, Edmund narrowly escaped riding over a precipice and vowed to restore Dunstan if he were saved. Keeping his vow, the king took Dunstan to Glastonbury, installed him as abbot, and promised royal support for the restoration of its worship and life.

At Glastonbury, Dunstan began a program of monastic renewal. He restored the ancient monastery, improved education, and strengthened the observance of the [*Rule of St. Benedict*](/meditations/the-rule-of-saint-benedict). This restoration would leave a lasting mark on English Christianity for generations. Dunstan then served successively as Bishop of Worcester and Bishop of London before being appointed Archbishop of Canterbury in 959. Soon after, he established a permanent monastic community on Thorney Island in London, laying the foundations of what would become Westminster Abbey. As archbishop, he worked to improve clerical discipline, encourage education, and promote greater liturgical unity throughout the kingdom. He even revised the coronation liturgies to reflect a deeply Christian understanding of kingship: rulers are accountable to God and called to govern with justice and humility. Within the monasteries, Dunstan encouraged monks to live in a spirit of self-sacrifice and forbade the selling of church positions for money. He often replaced secular canons with monks in the cathedrals and ensured that parish priests were qualified for their positions. He wanted in every way for the Church to be disciplined and to point toward Christ.

On the feast of the Ascension in the year 988, St. Dunstan officiated the Mass at Canterbury Cathedral. When he turned to give the benediction, he announced to the people that the end of his days were drawing near. After the Mass, he dined in the refectory, returned to the church, and pointed out the place where he desired to be laid. Three days later, he was no more. He was buried in the cathedral, and in 1180 his relics were translated to a tomb on the south side of the high altar.

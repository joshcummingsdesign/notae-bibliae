**Bishop** (461 AD)

[St. Leo the Great](/glossary/names#leo) was born in Tuscany and, after serving with distinction as a deacon of the Roman Church, was unanimously elected Bishop of Rome in 440 AD. A gifted pastor and theologian, he strengthened charitable works in Rome during times of famine and poverty, vigorously opposed heresies such as [Pelagianism](/glossary/historical-terms#pelagianism), [Manichaeism](/glossary/historical-terms#manichaeism), and [Priscillianism](/glossary/historical-terms#priscillianism), and promoted the primacy of the Roman See as a means of preserving the unity of the Church.

During the Christological controversies of the 5th century, Leo became one of the foremost defenders of the orthodox doctrine of Christ. In response to Eutyches—who taught that Christ possessed only one nature after the Incarnation, his human nature having been absorbed into his divinity—Leo wrote his celebrated *<Definition lang="english" anchor="tome" text="Tome" />* to Flavian, affirming that Christ is one Person in two natures, fully God and fully man. After careful examination, the Council of Chalcedon in 451 AD accepted Leo's teaching as consistent with the orthodox faith, affirming that the one Christ must be confessed in two natures, human and divine.

Leo also guided the Church through the political turmoil of the collapsing Western Roman Empire. In 452 AD he met Attila the Hun as part of an imperial embassy, and Attila subsequently withdrew from Italy. His papacy marked a decisive period in which the Bishop of Rome assumed an increasingly prominent role in both ecclesiastical and civil affairs.

Nearly one hundred sermons and one hundred fifty letters of St. Leo survive, focusing especially on the mystery of Christ and the dignity of the Christian life. He died in 461 AD and became the first pope to be buried within St. Peter's Basilica. He is remembered as one of the foremost bishops and theologians of the Latin Church.

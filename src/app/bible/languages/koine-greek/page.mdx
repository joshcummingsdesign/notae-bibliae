Ancient Greece had several regional dialects, including Ionic, Aeolic, and Attic, the dialect of Athens. The earliest Greek poets composed in a stylized blend of Old Ionic and other dialects, as did Homer and Hesiod, or in Aeolic, as did Sappho and Alcaeus. As Athens grew wealthy and powerful, it became a center of literature and philosophy, home to figures such as Sophocles, Euripides, Aristophanes, Socrates, Plato, and Aristotle. Their influence helped establish Attic as the leading dialect of the Greek-speaking world. The Macedonian kings adopted it, and Alexander the Great's conquests spread Greek from North Africa to the borders of India. The need for a common language among diverse peoples gave rise to <Definition lang="greek" anchor="koine" text="Koine" />, meaning "common", a form of Greek based mainly on Attic and related Ionic forms, with many of the language's complexities simplified. After Alexander's death, his empire fragmented, but Koine continued to spread, becoming the lingua franca of much of the Mediterranean and the Middle East.
